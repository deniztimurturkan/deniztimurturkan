Computer vision · Georgia Tech CS 4476

Labeling every pixel of a street scene

Before a self-driving car can decide what to do, it has to know what it's looking at. I trained a network that looks at a frame of driving footage and labels every pixel: road, sidewalk, car, pedestrian, pole, sky, and more.

Type
Individual course project
Timeline
Fall 2024
Data
CamVid driving video, 11 classes
Model
PSPNet, ResNet-50 backbone

The problem

Classifying a whole image ("this is a street") is the easy version. Semantic segmentation asks for a label on every single pixel, so the model has to know both what is in the scene and exactly where each thing ends. Thin objects like poles and signs, and small ones like pedestrians far away, are where models usually struggle.

The data was CamVid, 701 frames of driving footage from Cambridge labeled with 11 classes: building, tree, sky, car, sign, road, pedestrian, fence, pole, sidewalk, and bicyclist. Images were downsampled for training, which keeps most of the scene recognizable while making training practical on a single GPU.

The approach

I built the network up step by step, measuring mean intersection-over-union (mIoU) after each change to see what each idea was actually worth:

  • A simple segmentation net on a ResNet-50 backbone with a 1×1 convolution as the classifier, as a starting point.
  • ImageNet pretraining, so the backbone starts out already knowing edges, textures, and objects.
  • Data augmentation, randomly transforming the training images, since 701 images is a very small dataset.
  • Dilated convolutions, which let the network see a wider area without shrinking the image so much that thin objects like poles disappear.
  • A pyramid pooling module, PSPNet's key idea: pool features over the whole image and over 2×2, 3×3, and 6×6 grids, then combine them, so each pixel's label is informed by the scene around it. A car is more likely on the road than in the sky.
  • An auxiliary loss on an intermediate layer, to give the deep network a stronger training signal.

What makes it hard

Classes like road, sky, and buildings cover huge areas and are easy to get right. Poles, signs, pedestrians, and bicyclists cover few pixels and are often partly hidden, so a model can score well on average while still missing exactly the objects a car most needs to see. That's why per-class scores matter as much as the overall number.
