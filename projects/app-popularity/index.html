Predicting Google Play app popularity

Can you tell how popular an app will be from its store listing alone? We trained tree-based and nearest-neighbor models to find out, and learned as much from where they failed as from where they worked.

Role
Team member (team of 5)
Timeline
Spring 2024
Data
Google Play Store apps (Kaggle)
Tools
Python

The question

The app stores are saturated, and most new apps go nowhere. If listing details like category, size, and monetization could predict which apps will take off, that would help developers decide what to build and help investors decide what to back. We set out to test how far those details alone can go.

The data

We used a public Kaggle dataset of Google Play Store listings and built a single popularity score for each app by combining its number of installs with its average rating. To get the data ready, we:

  • handled missing values, and dropped apps with no usable size;
  • converted app size from text into a number;
  • encoded categorical fields such as category and content rating;
  • split the apps into training and test sets.

The features we kept were category, size, content rating, whether the app shows ads, and whether it has in-app purchases.

Results

We started with a decision tree and a random forest, then tried k-nearest neighbors. R² is the share of the variation in popularity a model explains: 1.0 would be perfect, 0 is no better than guessing the average.

ModelTest MSETest R²
Decision tree0.06110.045
Random forest0.05880.080
k-nearest neighbors (k = 19)—≈ 0.47

The k-NN R² is read off our tuning curve, so it is approximate.

The tree models had low error but explained under 10% of the variation. Their low error mostly came from the fact that most apps have low popularity scores, so predicting "not very popular" is usually close. Nearest neighbors did much better, explaining nearly half of the variation on the test set.

Tuning, and catching overfitting

  • Tree depth. Training error kept falling as trees got deeper, but test error bottomed out around a depth of 13 and then rose again. Past that point the tree was memorizing the training data, not learning from it.
  • Number of neighbors. With k = 1, the model scored about 0.92 R² on training data and almost 0 on test data, a textbook case of overfitting. As k grew, the two scores converged, and test R² climbed to about 0.47.

What I'd do next

Listing metadata alone only goes so far. The next steps we identified were gradient-boosted trees and a small neural network to capture nonlinear effects, richer features such as review text or update history, and a proper error analysis to see which kinds of apps the models get most wrong.
